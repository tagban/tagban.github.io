About

01

I'm a data engineer and BI analyst who ended up specializing in the handoff points other people avoid: migrating a company off spreadsheets and onto a real warehouse, reconciling data streams that were never meant to line up, and building AI workflows that hold up under HIPAA scrutiny instead of just demoing well.

Most recently I built the plan to transition The Health Institute from Airtable and Google Sheets storing data into a more robust data architecture with Snowflake, and before that spent five years at Ontrak Health owning ingestion automation, FHIR processing, and executive reporting for a healthcare operation with real compliance stakes. I'm equally comfortable in an executive review explaining why a number moved and in a terminal debugging why a pipeline didn't run.

That range is deliberate. I don't hand leadership a dashboard and walk away — I own the architecture decisions, defend them in the room, and make sure the people acting on the data actually understand why it says what it says. The build and the decision aren't two separate jobs to me; the second one doesn't work without the first.

01 · Clean the mess

Find where the inconsistency lives

Duplicate records, unstructured exports, timestamps that don't agree with each other — I track down the root cause and build the automation that keeps it from coming back, instead of patching the symptom each month.

02 · Model it for BI

Build data people stop questioning

Clean data becomes governed models and materialized views — the kind Power BI, and the executives reading it, can rely on without a disclaimer about which number is the real one.

03 · Make it AI-ready

The same rigor, pointed at AI

The discipline that makes a dashboard trustworthy is what makes a dataset safe to hand to an LLM: scoped to the right columns, de-identified where it has to be, and specific enough to be useful.

04 · Lead the decision

Make the case, not just the chart

A clean dataset doesn't decide anything by itself. I present the findings, defend the architecture choices, and make sure leadership understands the reasoning behind a number before they act on it — not just the number itself.
